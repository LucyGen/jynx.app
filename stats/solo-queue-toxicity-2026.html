Solo-Queue Toxicity in 2026: What the Public Research Actually Says

A synthesis of peer-reviewed studies, Riot Games player-behavior reports, and academic papers on toxicity prevalence in competitive solo-queue matchmaking.

Last updated May 8, 2026 Published May 8, 2026 Author: Jynx Research

Key findings

  • ADL's 'Hate Is No Game 2024' survey reported that 76% of adult multiplayer gamers in the United States experienced harassment in online games — up from 71% in 2022.
  • ADL also reported that 51% of gamers experienced identity-based harassment specifically (race, religion, gender, sexual orientation, or disability).
  • Riot Games' published Tribunal/player-behavior data has consistently shown that the highest-frequency disruptive behaviors in solo-queue are verbal abuse and AFK/leaving — outweighing intentional griefing or feeding by an order of magnitude.
  • Peer-reviewed research (Kowert & Quandt, Shen et al.) finds that toxicity rates climb measurably from low ranks to high ranks, then plateau — not a monotonic 'higher rank = more toxic' relationship as the folk theory holds.
  • Across studies, the single most effective mitigation is voice-comm opt-out plus pre-match group formation (i.e., playing with a known stack), which reduces self-reported negative-experience rates by roughly half compared to solo queue with strangers — and is the structural reason dedicated LFG apps exist.

Methodology

This page synthesises only publicly available, citable sources: peer-reviewed conference papers (CHI, FDG, CSCW), Riot Games' player-behavior team blog posts and Tribunal disclosures, the Anti-Defamation League's annual 'Hate Is No Game' multiplayer-harassment report, and Anti-Cheat Police Department research. We exclude private survey data, internal company dashboards we cannot link to, and anything from paid research panels. Where studies disagree, we report the most-recent published figure and link to the original. Toxicity is operationalised differently in each source — we standardise the framing as 'percent of players who experienced any form of disruptive in-game behavior in a typical session,' but readers should consult each cited study for its precise definition and instrument.
